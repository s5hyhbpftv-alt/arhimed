**Day 1 Contest Analysis**

Prior to the test, I decide to scan the problems in the double-edged ![Image 63: $321$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/9/6/9/9691986bc20f256cdd5f2a7c316ddaa8db873e6b.png) order. This gives you as much time as possible to crack the hardest problem, but can put you in serious time pressure if things don't go well. Problem ![Image 64: $3$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/7/c/d/7cde695f2e4542fd01f860a89189f47a27143b66.png)'s statement catches my eye, but half an hour of mucking around doesn't yield much more than ![Image 65: $c=\tfrac{2}{3}$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/4/c/8/4c8994705580179c7dc98b1080bcbf234c8efad2.png) when ![Image 66: $n=1$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/d/9/1d96ecc15129c5631ba4a2c6578e91e3d13e3baf.png). Now it's time to extrapolate and guess the answer. I realise that the "deficit" ![Image 67: $D = 2c - 1$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/2/3/f/23f98daadd8a1c4a6bdb2b26f8638bc5aaf6af3f.png) is an important quantity, but ![Image 68: $D(0) = 1$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/b/2/7/b27a75de53e8d594153cf68d78af9831068f5aa1.png) and ![Image 69: $D(1) = \tfrac{1}{3}$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/e/e/4/ee49acd9b18ee46b6038768c0976e27d61dbb73f.png) lead me to guessing ![Image 70: $D(n) = \tfrac{1}{3^n}$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/6/c/a/6ca458ae2d7f069a2463d2b1d34ff22fa472eb13.png). Exponential decay, but far from the correct answer of ![Image 71: $D(n) = \tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}-1}$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/c/e/9/ce9d7daf23f7420ee8faf1344849fc6a54a12d02.png).

It's time to get something concrete done, so I draw a decent diagram for ![Image 72: $\#2$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/5/0/a/50a3fce09f255c10f3ac2baf26960532b59423d9.png). The angle conditions are weird and quickly dispel possible bashes that come to mind. I stare at the picture for a while. I introduce ![Image 79: $C_1$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/7/0/b/70b97eb4ad6e4e7a5a97e2547d9ed6e965960d0c.png) analogously, and soon have ![Image 80: $3$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/7/c/d/7cde695f2e4542fd01f860a89189f47a27143b66.png) cyclic quadrilaterals which use up the angle conditions.

Opening the paper from its envelope, I'm jumpscared by ![Image 118: $\#6$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/2/c/12c4f0024861c6f0e50a868a042a0397593c07a3.png) not being geometry, and don't fully appreciate how cute the statement is yet. I try doing small cases with ![Image 119: $a_1 = 15$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/3/a/6/3a6683dffa5444800e0888d5aa66d608a19e587d.png), and end up writing about ![Image 120: $20$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/f/8/3/f8366cd6196dd8a9da6d38a3e9eafb109e99d53e.png) terms of the sequence with ![Image 121: $a_1 = 105$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/e/1/1/e11feeaa9c78aeae9a6c3b6fe44137a1ceaedb75.png). The final condition just says that the membership of an integer in the sequence is dependent only on the remainders it leaves modulo a finite set of prime numbers.

![Image 122: https://i.postimg.cc/66M18Ztd/P6-rough-compressed.png](https://cdn.aops.com/images/0/2/8/0285144aecf80a44969a06ae2bcd54a4ed5e1837.png)

_An excerpt from my rough work for ![Image 123: $\#6$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/2/c/12c4f0024861c6f0e50a868a042a0397593c07a3.png)_

Twenty minutes in, I have a proper look at ![Image 124: $\#5$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/9/d/19d1a0132e656edad2bed80eabda7c359a7e60c9.png), and incorrectly conjecture that only the identity function works. I arbitrarily substitute ![Image 125: $x = f(y)$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/e/3/7/e37447b17a50be685e7f85fd62ad10410185c37c.png) and get that ![Image 126: $x, f(x), f(f(x)), \dots$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/6/2/a/62a817d2e55f96b0d561a43d2bfc49248880880a.png) forms an arithmetic progression. Never mind, ![Image 127: $f(x) \equiv x + c$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/f/d/6/fd65eacc4b0fbade7c7ccf0ddefeae2aecf7a1cb.png) works too. Now to show that ![Image 128: $g(x) := f(x) - x$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/2/d/a/2da1fa1ec309f8f84c208c6fb695ce84cf06288d.png) is constant: if ![Image 129: $g(x)$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/6/5/9/659a6a2610357101d77dbae8eb9bf0b3100c08fd.png) and ![Image 130: $g(y)$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/c/8/7/c87ffb791483f5eb7b7cfe68f44c77bbfdf728dd.png) are distinct and nonzero, "lifting" ![Image 131: $x$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/2/6/e/26eeb5258ca5099acf8fe96b2a1049c48c89a5e6.png) and ![Image 132: $y$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/0/9/2/092e364e1d9d19ad5fffb0b46ef4cc7f2da02c1c.png) using the progression while keeping them close together breaks the inequality. A final pointwise trap resolves itself, and I verify that the claimed solution set works. (Read: apply QM-AM-GM.) Once the exam is finished, I hear this problem admits a cool calculus based approach too.

I had registered ![Image 133: $\#4$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/6/2/162f83580ee29c982f2730580bc839d8eb4901fd.png) as an interesting statement earlier, except for the unnecessary flavour text. First, I prove that ![Image 134: $\theta = 90^\circ$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/6/7/2/67227c5501c8a0fcefd2a2acd2c0a370a5cb8583.png) works and obtuse angles don't. Doodling and trying (in vain) to force all ![Image 135: $\theta \leq 90^\circ$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/f/8/6/f8653aa29405f068ee2ae82bd4583f5a5a717c87.png) to work gives a bunch of angles measuring ![Image 136: $2\theta$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/0/8/2/08221292fc5357dd032d4c088bd58915c3707f08.png) and ![Image 137: $3\theta$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/f/3/4/f34abb1905779a6fa2c98b8994448b09bd6a213e.png). This leads to calling integer multiples of ![Image 138: $\theta$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/5/2/e/52e8ed7a3ba22130ad3984eb2cd413406475a689.png) "good" and all other angles "bad," which extends to a solution. By the time I finish writing up, the problem has really grown on me.

With two hours to work on ![Image 139: $\#6$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/2/c/12c4f0024861c6f0e50a868a042a0397593c07a3.png), I look at the big picture from scratch, and work with the sets ![Image 140: $S_n$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/c/7/4/c74a070eea97d9363f58a257e05e75fc69efa753.png) of prime divisors of ![Image 141: $a_n$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/6/f/1/6f1a2b6b7c193f9ba2b2134a59f1da5addcfbc98.png). The first hour or so of scouting doesn't yield much. By this stage, I've started causing deforestation in China due to all the extra sheets of paper I've requested for rough work. Eventually, I hit on the following definition of "good" sets: ![Image 142: $S$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/a/d/2/ad28c83c99a8fd0dd2e2e594c9d02ee532765a0a.png) is good if ![Image 143: $S = S_n$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/e/3/6/e36e628ac42fbe588b1f75b779687111d9ec3177.png) for some ![Image 144: $n$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/7/4/174fadd07fd54c9afe288e96558c92e0c1da733a.png), and ![Image 145: $S_i \not\subseteq S$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/2/2/9/229c9af8e711558abcc8f65ba654abe95d2fbe38.png) for all ![Image 146: $i < n$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/9/e/5/9e5693ebf90472eecaebb529f886a35474d27819.png). Now the problem just asks us to show good sets are bounded! I write the claim "If ![Image 147: $q$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/0/6/1/0615acc3725de21025457e7d6f7694dab8e2f758.png) lies in a good set, then ![Image 148: $q > M$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/d/2/6/d262bb3527ab76bdf940675e08f7137536692f4a.png)", and leave space to define a constant ![Image 149: $M$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/5/d/1/5d1e4485dc90c450e8c76826516c1b2ccb8fce16.png) later. A bound by taking minimal counterexamples gives ![Image 150: $M = a_1p_k$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/0/e/2/0e2c552175b61b0bbfe00c1c30520026229f28b2.png), where ![Image 151: $p_k$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/4/5/0/4508393135451dc6c41b01f36072dd16674586bc.png) is the largest prime factor of ![Image 152: $a_1$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/1/a/b/1ab39d761413804680d26d972381f028001562f5.png).

All that's left is to organize these ideas chronologically and make sure they don't break, but the time pressure is starting to get to me. I have a snack to compose myself. With about five minutes to go, a haphazardly written Problem ![Image 153: $6$](https://latex.artofproblemsolving.com/6/0/1/601a7806cbfad68196c43a4665871f8c3186802e.png) solution is on my desk. Relief.
